From Ticks to Memory

How circuits keep time, and how keeping time lets them remember.

Scroll or press →

1. Why keep time

Signals arrive at different moments

Wires have different lengths. Some parts are slower than others.

Read too early and you get a mix of old and new.

1. Why keep time

A clock says: read now

Everyone waits for the tick.

Between ticks, signals settle. On the tick, everyone reads together.

2. Timing with a bucket

A bucket and a narrow pipe

A capacitor stores charge, like a bucket.

A resistor slows the flow, like a narrow pipe.

Filling takes time. That delay is how a circuit measures time.

The screen is like a heart monitor. Higher means more voltage. Further right means later.

2. Timing with a bucket

The time constant, τ

τ = R × C

A narrower pipe or a bigger bucket means a slower fill.

After 1 τ it is 63% full. After 5 τ it is basically full.

10 kΩ × 100 µF = 1 second.

3. The 555 timer

The 555 repeats it

A cheap and famous chip from 1972.

At 2/3 full it opens a drain. At 1/3 full it closes it.

Fill, drain, fill, drain. A steady beat.

3. The 555 timer

Inside the chip

Two watchers compare the bucket to 2/3 and 1/3.

They flip a tiny memory, a flip-flop, which works the drain.

Remember that flip-flop. It comes back later.

3. The 555 timer

Choosing the speed

f ≈ 1.44 ÷ (C × (R1 + 2 R2))

Bigger parts give a slower beat.

Great for blinking lights and buzzers. Too wobbly for a computer.

4. The clock

Keep only the flips

The output pin is on while filling and off while draining.

High means 1. Low means 0.

Each jump up is a tick. This is a clock.

4. The clock

Real clocks use quartz

A quartz crystal vibrates 32,768 times a second, very steadily.

32,768 is 2 multiplied by itself 15 times.

15 flip-flops each halve the beat. Out comes exactly 1 tick per second.

4. The clock

How fast is fast?

A laptop ticks about 3 billion times a second. That is 3 GHz.

A circuit called a PLL multiplies a slower, steady clock up to that speed.

One tick lasts 0.33 ns. Light only travels 10 cm in that time.

5. Gates

A transistor is a switch

A small voltage on its gate lets current through.

No voltage, no current.

A modern chip holds billions of them.

5. Gates

Gates make decisions

A few transistors make a logic gate.

NOT flips its input.

NOR outputs 1 only when both inputs are 0.

6. The SR latch

Two gates in a loop

Each NOR gate feeds its output into the other.

Press SET and follow the steps.

Let go, and the loop holds Q at 1. That is memory.

6. The SR latch

RESET clears it

Press RESET and Q drops to 0.

Let go, and it stays 0.

6. The SR latch

Never press both

Q and Q should always be opposites.

Pressing both breaks that rule. Let go of both and it is a coin toss.

7. Latch vs flip-flop

D latch: an open door

D is the data. CLK decides when to listen.

While CLK is high the door is open. Q copies D, even as D changes.

7. Latch vs flip-flop

D flip-flop: a camera flash

It copies D only at the rising edge, the instant CLK goes up.

Then it holds, whatever D does.

Computers use flip-flops so everything changes in step.

8. Bits to bytes

Eight bits make a byte

One flip-flop holds one bit, a 0 or a 1.

Eight in a row hold a byte. 01000001 is 65, which computers use for the letter A.

8. Bits to bytes

Registers

64 flip-flops make a 64-bit register.

The processor keeps the numbers it is working on in these.

On every tick, they all update together.

9. The cost of a bit

Why not flip-flops everywhere?

A flip-flop needs about 20 transistors for one bit.

An SRAM cell needs 6. A DRAM cell needs just 1, plus a tiny capacitor.

9. The cost of a bit

Billions of bits

16 GB of RAM is about 128 billion bits.

As flip-flops, that would be over 2.5 trillion transistors.

So we trade some speed for size.

10. DRAM, your computer's RAM

One tap and one tiny bucket

Full bucket means 1. Empty means 0.

The word line opens a whole row of taps at once. The bit lines carry the charge.

10. DRAM, your computer's RAM

The bucket leaks

The charge slowly escapes.

Wait too long and a 1 looks like a 0.

10. DRAM, your computer's RAM

So it refreshes

Every 64 ms, each row is read and topped up.

Fill, leak, fill, leak. Like the 555, it never stops.

10. DRAM, your computer's RAM

Reading empties it

Reading spills the charge onto the bit line.

A sense amplifier decides 0 or 1, then writes it back.

Power off, and the data fades away. This is called volatile.

11. NAND flash, your SSD

Electrons in a sealed pocket

A flash cell has a pocket wrapped in insulation.

Electrons trapped inside change how the cell behaves.

No power needed. They stay for years. This is called non-volatile.

11. NAND flash, your SSD

Writing wears it out

About 20 V pushes electrons through the insulation. This is called tunneling.

Each trip damages it a little.

11. NAND flash, your SSD

Pages and blocks

You write one page at a time.

You can only erase a whole block.

The SSD's controller shuffles data around to spread the wear.

11. NAND flash, your SSD

More bits per cell

A TLC cell holds 8 charge levels. That is 3 bits.

Modern chips stack over 200 layers of cells.

Dense and cheap, but slow to write.

Recap

From ticks to memory

  1. A capacitor filling through a resistor turns charge into time.
  2. A clock turns time into ticks, so every part acts together.
  3. Gates in a loop hold a bit. A flip-flop stores it on the tick.
  4. Flip-flops are fast, DRAM is dense, and flash lasts. Each trades speed, size and power.
