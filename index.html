Electronic Timing Circuit Memory

How circuits keep time, and how keeping time lets them remember.

Scroll or press →

1. Why keep time

Signals arrive at different times

Some wires are longer. Some parts are slower.

Read too early and you get a mix of old and new.

1. Why keep time

A clock says: read now

Everyone waits for the tick, like a band waiting for the drum.

On the tick, everyone reads together.

2. Timing with a bucket

A bucket and a narrow pipe

A capacitor stores electricity, like a bucket stores water.

A resistor slows the flow, like a narrow pipe.

Filling takes time. That is how a circuit measures time.

2. Timing with a bucket

Bigger bucket, slower fill

A bigger bucket or a narrower pipe takes longer to fill.

Pick the parts, and you pick the delay.

3. The 555 timer

Fill, drain, repeat

The 555 is a cheap and famous timer chip.

At 2/3 full it drains the bucket. At 1/3 full it fills it again.

Over and over. A steady beat.

3. The 555 timer

Inside the chip

Two watchers keep an eye on the bucket.

They flip a tiny memory, a flip-flop, that works the drain.

Remember that flip-flop. It comes back later.

4. The clock

Keep only on and off

The output is on while filling and off while draining.

On means 1. Off means 0.

Each jump up is a tick. That is a clock.

4. The clock

Real clocks use quartz

A tiny quartz crystal shakes 32,768 times a second, very steadily.

That is a power of two. Halve it 15 times and you get exactly 1 tick per second.

4. The clock

Billions of ticks a second

A laptop ticks about 3 billion times every second.

Each tick is so short that light barely crosses your hand.

5. Gates

A transistor is a switch

It is a switch flipped by electricity, not by a finger.

Power on its gate lets current through.

A modern chip holds billions of them.

5. Gates

Gates make simple choices

A few transistors make a logic gate.

NOT flips its input.

NOR gives 1 only when both inputs are 0.

6. The SR latch

Two gates in a loop

Each gate feeds its output into the other.

Press SET, then let go.

The loop keeps Q at 1. That is memory.

6. The SR latch

RESET clears it

Press RESET and Q drops to 0.

Let go, and it stays 0.

6. The SR latch

Never press both

Q and Q should always be opposites.

Press both and that breaks. Let go of both and it is a coin toss.

7. Latch vs flip-flop

D latch: an open door

D is the data. CLK is the clock.

While CLK is high the door is open, and Q copies D the whole time.

7. Latch vs flip-flop

D flip-flop: a camera flash

It copies D only at the instant CLK goes up.

Then it holds, whatever D does.

So every part of a computer changes in step.

8. Bits to bytes

Eight bits make a byte

One flip-flop holds one bit.

Eight of them hold a byte. 01000001 is 65, which computers use for the letter A.

8. Bits to bytes

Registers

A register is the processor's notepad.

64 flip-flops side by side hold one 64-bit number.

On every tick, they all update together.

9. The cost of a bit

Flip-flops are big

A flip-flop needs about 20 transistors to hold one bit.

Your RAM holds billions of bits, so it needs something smaller.

DRAM uses 1 transistor and a tiny bucket.

10. DRAM, your computer's RAM

A tap and a tiny bucket

Each bit is a tiny bucket with a tap.

Full means 1. Empty means 0.

A whole row of taps opens at once.

10. DRAM, your computer's RAM

The bucket leaks

The charge slowly escapes.

Wait too long and a 1 looks like a 0.

10. DRAM, your computer's RAM

So it keeps topping up

Many times a second, every row is filled back up.

A timer, like the 555, says when.

Fill, leak, fill, leak. It never stops.

10. DRAM, your computer's RAM

Reading empties it

Reading spills the bucket, so the chip fills it back.

Turn the power off and everything is forgotten.
